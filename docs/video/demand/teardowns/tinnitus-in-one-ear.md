## Why these

The 10-28 `outliers "tinnitus in one ear"` page: six standouts across three
channels, all on one promise - *is this serious, and when do I act*. Treble's
"Should you ring the alarm" is the long form with the most views and the
cleanest match to the article's own red-flag section.

## The promise

Whether one-sided ringing is a warning sign, and what to do about it. The
viewer has noticed the sound sits on one side, has probably read that it can
mean something serious, and is frightened. Problem-aware, looking for a
verdict on *their* case. "Ring the alarm" names the fear in the title, and the
answer is neither yes nor no - it is "get it checked".

## The opening (promise / proof / plan)

- **Promise at 0:05**: the question to the viewer ("ringing in just one
  side?"), then the medical name.
- **Proof at 0:00**: a doctor of audiology, said first. We cannot have this.
- **Plan at ~0:25**: causes, treatment, tests. Then a subscribe ask, a
  clinic plug and a "what is tinnitus" recap, and **the heatmap is near zero
  from 0:18 to 0:42** - the plan and the plug are exactly what people skip.

## The structure

1,657 words at 167 wpm, nine chapters. The order: what tinnitus is, one side
is a "red flag", what to tell the doctor, what the doctor does, then a
**numbered list of five causes** (infection, sudden hearing loss, a balance
condition, a rare benign tumour, a loud event), then the appointment, then
treatment and a sound-therapy product. The turn is at ~6:15: "for all of
those there are treatments - the first step is to get diagnosed". Ends on a
clinic CTA.

## What satisfied viewers

- Most comments are a personal case asking for a verdict: "I just want to
  know if I'm going to go deaf or it's just ear wax", normal hearing test
  "but still ringing", a jaw broken on that side, water in the ear. **They
  want to sort their own case into a bucket.** A video that gives them the
  buckets and the one sign that changes the urgency is the thing.
- Asked-for and missing: jaw and neck causes, Eustachian tube. Our article
  has jaw.
- "This was just one big infomercial" - the plug is the warning.
- A 16-like comment: reading other people's worsening stories raised their
  stress. The video's calm is part of what it is for.
- On the "few seconds" video: relief at "basically nothing to worry about".
  A brief, fading one-sided ring is common; our article does not cover it,
  so we do not either.

## Our version - keep, cannot have, replaced by, rule limits

- **Source:** `tinnitus-in-one-ear.mdx` (2026-10-04). It is built on the
  AAO-HNSF clinical practice guideline and the NIDCD sudden-deafness page.
- **Keep:** the fear named as the question; the causes as buckets, split
  *common* and *less common* (the peak); the one sign that changes the
  urgency; "get it checked" as the verdict; a calm, short close.
- **Cannot have:** a doctor on camera, a clinic to send people to.
- **Replaced by:** the guideline itself as the proof - its page captured
  and the sentence ringed. That is stronger than a credential for this
  audience: it is what *every* clinician is told to do. The buckets drawn
  (`grid`), the urgency as a `dial` (routine test -> same day), the red flags
  as a `checklist`.
- **Rule limits:** no diagnosis and no "it's probably nothing". The article's
  own line: usually ordinary, *and* the one pattern guidelines flag for a
  prompt hearing test - both said. The tumour is named once, as rare, as the
  article does, never as the hook. Sudden hearing loss on that side is the
  one same-day sign, said plainly. No treatment promise; "hearing aids can
  reduce the perceived volume" stays the article's wording or is dropped.
- **Skip what they spent the front on:** no "what is tinnitus" recap, no
  plan read aloud, no plug. The question, then the verdict, by 0:10.
- **Length:** ~2:30-3:00 long form against 9:54; the Short does one move -
  one side is the pattern they check, and the one sign that makes it today.
