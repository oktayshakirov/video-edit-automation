## The promise

"There is no cure, but here are the four things that do help." It answers
the question first and refuses the false one ("cure? no"), then promises a
short, numbered list. The viewer is problem-aware and solution-hungry: they
have searched for hours and been told nothing can be done. The thumbnail
carries the number.

## The opening (promise / proof / plan, with times)

- **Promise, 0:00-0:40:** names the viewer's search and the "nothing can be
  done" they were told, and contradicts it.
- **Proof, ~1:35:** the presenter has tinnitus himself and is an audiologist
  (a clinic, a patient count on Treble's version). We have neither.
- **Plan, 1:03:** "the causes, then the best researched methods." The list
  itself does not start until 9:50 - over half the video is causes. The
  heatmap shows the opening minute nearly unwatched (0.00-0.01 at 0:20-1:01):
  people skip the preamble.

Treble's opens better: the claim ("I ranked every treatment"), the tension
("treatments with dozens of trials, and treatments with zero evidence that
millions pay for"), the four tiers on screen by 1:02, and a held-back tease
("the strongest one surprises almost everyone").

## The structure (chapters, points, length, wpm, the turn, the CTA)

17:02, 184 wpm, 14 chapters. Four points: get a hearing test (and hearing
aids if there is loss), sound enrichment, CBT / mindfulness, knowledge. Then
when to see a doctor. The turn is at 13:27: the physical damage often stays,
but the reaction to it is what can change - the habituation idea.

Treble's: 22:00, 18 treatments in four tiers, worst tier first, best last
(insufficient evidence -> specific subtypes -> proven -> top tier).

## Rewatched and skipped (heatmap)

Peaks at 2:44-2:54 (the presenter's own tinnitus played as a sound) and
13:07-13:17 (the sound-enrichment samples, played aloud). **People rewatch
the moment they can hear something.** The trough is the whole opening
minute.

## What satisfied viewers (comments)

Thanks for "clear and accurate information" and for not being told "nothing
can be done". Several comments are people's own stories. One complains that
every method failed "aside from the possible gimmick dietary supplements" -
the supplement question is on their minds. On Treble's, **the top five
comments are complaints about background music under the voice** ("the music
is making my tinnitus worse"), and one calls it "monetization of tinnitus"
(it sells Lenire in the pinned comment).

## The failures beside it

Treble's tier list, the stronger format on paper, sits at ~1.5x: 22 minutes,
music under the voice, a product sold in the comments. The guideline videos
on our exact term are small (Timpanogos ~3x, everything else tiny), so the
term has few answers - a fresh-term chance, not a proven page.

## Our version - keep, cannot have, replaced by, rule limits

- **Keep:** the "no cure, but here is what the evidence supports" honesty
  up front; a numbered set of tiers on screen early (by ~0:20, not 9:50);
  worst first, best last; a sound heard once, in the sound-enrichment tier.
- **Cannot have:** an audiologist on camera, his own tinnitus, a clinic's
  patient count.
- **Replaced by:** the guideline itself as the authority - the 2026 AAO-HNSF
  clinical practice guideline, its page on screen in a `screencap`, its own
  strength grades as our tiers. We are not ranking; the guideline's panel
  did, and we show it.
- **Rule limits:** tiers name the strength of the recommendation, never
  "works / doesn't". No relief promised for any tier. "Advised against" is
  about recommending a treatment *for tinnitus*, never "stop taking your
  medication" (the article's own "primary indication" caveat goes in). The
  warning signs that still need prompt care close the video. **No music bed
  under the narration beyond our usual low level** - Treble's comments are
  the evidence that this audience hears it.
- **Length:** E26 window, 5-8 minutes. Not 17 or 22.
